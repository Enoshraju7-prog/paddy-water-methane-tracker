# 12 August 2026 — the day the method failed, on purpose

*Written for someone who has never touched a satellite. No jargon that isn't
explained on the spot.*

---

## The one-sentence version

We went to the fields at 6 in the morning, 15 minutes after a satellite flew
overhead, photographed 12 places holding water — and then found that our
software said all 12 were dry.

That sounds bad. It is the most useful day the project has had.

---

## What we were trying to do

Rice fields sit under water for months. Underwater mud has no oxygen, and
without oxygen the bacteria in it produce **methane** — a greenhouse gas about
27 times worse than CO₂ for warming, per tonne.

Fewer flooded days means less methane. So if we can count the flooded days from
space, we can measure how much methane a field emits, and how much it would save
if the farmer let it dry out occasionally.

The whole project rests on one question: **can a satellite tell whether a field
has water in it?**

---

## How the satellite is supposed to work

The satellite is **Sentinel-1**. It doesn't take photographs. It fires a radar
pulse at the ground and listens for the echo. That matters here because this is
monsoon season — an ordinary camera in orbit would spend four months
photographing the tops of clouds. Radar goes straight through cloud and works at
night.

The idea we started with is simple and, in a textbook, correct:

> **Still water acts like a mirror.** The pulse hits the water surface and
> bounces *away* from the satellite instead of back to it. So almost no echo
> comes back and flooded ground shows up **dark**.

Dark = wet. Bright = dry. We set the dividing line at a number called **−16 dB**
(decibels — the unit for how loud the echo is). Below the line, we call it
flooded.

---

## What we actually did today

The satellite passed over at **05:52 in the morning**. Enosh was standing in the
fields at **06:07** — fifteen minutes later — and walked for about an hour and a
quarter, taking 52 photographs.

Every photograph carries a GPS tag, so we know exactly where each one was taken
to within a few metres. That matters: on a trip the day before, the camera's GPS
was switched off and the locations had to be reconstructed afterwards from map
links. Those locations were guesses. Today's are measurements.

We grouped the 52 photos into **12 locations** and pulled the satellite's reading
for each one.

Every single one of those 12 places had **5 to 7 centimetres of standing water**
in it, clearly visible in the photographs. The farmers, asked directly, said the
water is present for the *entire* growing season — they never drain it.

So the correct answer is: **12 out of 12 flooded.**

---

## The result

| What the software said | Score |
|---|---|
| Our original rule | **0 out of 12** |

Not close. Not "needs tuning". Zero.

---

## Why it failed — and this is the interesting part

The textbook is right that **open water** is dark. But a rice field is not open
water. It is thousands of **stems standing up out of the water**.

Picture the corner where a wall meets the floor. Throw a ball into that corner
and it comes straight back at you. A rice stem meeting the water surface makes
exactly that shape.

So the radar pulse hits the water, bounces sideways into a stem, and is sent
**straight back up to the satellite**. This is called **double bounce**.

The result is the opposite of what we assumed:

- A **bare, just-flooded** field, before the plants grow — genuinely dark ✅
- A **flooded field full of growing rice** — **bright** ❌

Our rule wasn't measuring "is there water". It was measuring "is this field in
the two-week window right after planting, before the plants come up". For the
other four months of the season it was blind.

---

## What we tried next

If flooded fields are bright, then don't ask *"how dark is it?"* — ask
*"how much darker has it got compared to how this same field looked in March,
when it was bare?"*

Every field gets compared to its own dry-season self. That should cancel out the
fact that one field is naturally brighter than its neighbour.

We also switched from **VV** to **VH**. These are just two ways the radar listens
— VH is the one that picks up signal bouncing around inside leaves, and it turns
out to react much more strongly when a paddy floods.

| Rule | Score |
|---|---|
| Original: VV darker than −16 dB | 0 / 12 |
| Simply: VH darker than −16 dB | **8 / 12** |
| The clever version: VH dropped from its own March level | **8 / 12** |

**The clever version was no better than the simple one.** We tested 17 different
settings for it. None beat 8 out of 12.

And the **same four locations** fail under every rule we tried. They are all in
the southern half of the walk — and looking back at the year of satellite data,
those are the same four whose readings never showed the dip that the others did
when they were planted.

So the next question is not *"what number should the threshold be?"*. It is
**"what is different about those four fields?"**

---

## Other things settled today

**There is no winter rice crop here.** We pulled 20 months of satellite data,
back to December 2024, looking for a second flooding each winter. There isn't
one. This matters more than it sounds: the international methane formula has a
multiplier for whether the field was flooded in the months *before* the season,
and it is either 1.0 or 2.41. Getting this wrong would have **more than doubled**
every number the project produces. It's 1.0.

**The season starts a month later than we assumed.** Our settings said 15 June.
The satellite curves and the farmers both say mid-July.

**The crop is longer than we assumed.** The formula was using 120 days. The
farmers say planting was late June and harvest is late November or mid-December
— that's 150 to 173 days. Methane scales directly with this, so it's 25–44% we
were leaving out.

**We found a faster source of satellite data.** We had been using Microsoft's
copy of the archive. It turns out the European Space Agency publishes to its own
system within hours, while Microsoft's copy lagged more than a day. For a project
built on *same-day* checking, that difference is the whole point. Both are now
wired up.

**We fixed two bugs.** One made the program crash the second time you ran it.
The other made it fail *silently* — it would finish, report success, and produce
nothing at all. Three of our twelve locations disappeared that way before anyone
noticed.

---

## Why a failure is the right outcome

It would have been easy to move the −16 line until the numbers looked good. The
chart would have been beautiful and the project would have been worthless,
because the number would have been tuned to agree rather than measured to be
true.

Instead there is now a **scoreboard**. The farmers confirmed water is present all
season, so the correct answer for every satellite pass during the season is
"flooded". That means any pass our software calls "dry" is a mistake we can
count, exactly, with no argument.

Right now the score is 8 out of 12. It is written down. Every future change gets
measured against it.

An honest 67% that you can improve beats a flattering 95% that you can't trust.

## Where the project stands

The plan has 14 days of work. Days 1–5 — foundations, field visit, data plumbing
— are done. **Day 6–7 is flood detection, and that is where we are.** The plan
says, in its own words, that if this isn't working by Day 7 you stop adding
features and fix it. So that's what happens next.

Nothing after this — the emissions model, the web page, the phone app — is worth
building until a satellite can reliably tell whether a field has water in it.
